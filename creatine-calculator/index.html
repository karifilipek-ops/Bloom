How this is calculated

Why not just 5 g? The classic 3–5 g a day was set for an average-sized person. Research now doses creatine by body weight, about 0.1 g per kg a day (Antonio et al., 2021), with studies using 0.03–0.14 g/kg. So bigger and more active people need more.

Your range is based on your lean body mass, since almost all of your creatine is stored in muscle. It works out to about 0.07–0.1 g per kg of body weight for someone around 20% body fat, a bit more if you’re leaner and a bit less if you carry more fat. Never under 5 g. More weekly training moves your sweet spot toward the top.

Adjustments: +2 g if you eat mostly plant-based (lower starting stores). If you’re 50+, the low end goes up. For brain benefits the range goes up about 3–5 g: the brain takes up creatine slowly, and studies that raised brain levels used higher doses (Candow et al., 2023). The top of the range is capped at 12 g a day.

Safety: creatine is one of the most-studied supplements and is well tolerated in healthy people, including long term.

Will creatine make me bloated or “watery”?

Short answer:
Not the way you think.

Creatine pulls water into your muscle cells.
Not under your skin.

So yes, the scale might go up 1–3 lbs in the first few weeks (a bit more if you load).
But that’s water stored inside the muscle, which is part of why your muscles look fuller, not puffier.

So instead of looking bloated, it actually makes you look leaner and more defined.

If you’re worried about it, just skip the loading phase. Taking your daily dose from the start gets you to the same place, just a little slower.

Does creatine cause hair loss?

This myth comes from one poorly done study back in 2009 that didn’t even measure hair loss…

Researchers saw DHT (a hormone linked to hair loss) go up in a group of rugby players taking creatine.
And no one has been able to repeat that result since.

A massive review of the research done in 2021 concluded that creatine doesn’t increase testosterone, DHT, or cause hair loss or baldness.

If you’re genetically going bald, creatine isn’t what’s doing it.

Is creatine bad for my kidneys?

Short answer:
No.

Creatine is one of the most-studied supplements on the planet, and in healthy people it does not harm your kidneys. Not short term, and not when taken for years.

Where the confusion comes from:
Your body breaks creatine down into something called creatinine.
And creatinine is what doctors check on bloodwork to see how your kidneys are doing.

So taking creatine can bump that number up a bit, which can look like a kidney problem on paper, even when there isn’t one.

Just tell your doctor you take creatine before you get bloodwork done.

The only exception: if you already have kidney disease, check with your doctor first (same as you would with any supplement).

When should I take it, and do I need to load?

Creatine isn’t like caffeine.
It doesn’t “hit” when you take it.

It works by slowly topping up the creatine stored in your muscles over time.
So when you take it barely matters.
That you take it every day (rest days included) is what matters.

Morning, pre-workout, post-workout, in your coffee…
Pick whatever time you’ll actually remember.

As for loading:
It just gets you fully saturated in about a week instead of 3–4.
Same end result, just faster.

If it upsets your stomach, take it with a meal or split it into 2 doses.

Stop worrying about the pebbles (timing)
And focus on the boulders (taking it consistently)

Do I need to cycle off creatine?

No.

There’s no research showing that taking breaks from creatine does anything for you.

Your body doesn’t “build a tolerance” to it, and it doesn’t stop working over time.

And if you do stop for a while, your body’s own creatine production goes right back to normal.

So you can just take it every day, year-round.

Does the type of creatine matter?

Creatine HCl, buffered creatine, liquid creatine, creatine gummies…

Companies love to make it sound complicated.

But plain old creatine monohydrate is the most studied, has the most proof that it works, and is the cheapest.

None of the fancy forms have been shown to work better, they just cost more.

And be careful with gummies: some independent lab tests have found gummies with way less creatine in them than the label says.

If you want an extra layer of trust, look for a third-party tested monohydrate (like one that’s NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport).
